Course Skeleton

One chain of cause and effect runs through every lecture of this course. This page shows the chain, the workflow you will use to read it from the rocks, and how the exam and the weekly assignments work.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the workflow starts from observed stacking patterns and not from the controls or from a model template

Part I: The Genetic Chain

1. One question, one chain

The question of the course was set in Lecture 1: why does a depositional system move basinward or landward through time? The answer is a chain of cause and effect. Every topic in this course is one link of it.

At the shoreline, two rates compete: the rate at which accommodation is created or destroyed, and the rate of sedimentation. Accommodation is the space made available for sediment to fill (Jervey, 1988); it is created or destroyed by changes in relative sea level, that is eustasy combined with subsidence or uplift, and it is measured independently of sedimentation (Catuneanu, 2019a). The balance of the two rates decides the direction of the shoreline. The path of the shoreline through time, the shoreline trajectory, decides which stratal stacking pattern is built. Changes in stacking pattern are what we map as sequence stratigraphic surfaces. Each stacking pattern, bounded by those surfaces, is a systems tract. A full cycle of change in stacking pattern is a sequence. The Wheeler diagram shows the same record with time, instead of thickness, on the vertical axis.

2. Each link, by when, where and why

LinkWhat happensWhat it does to the record
1 Allogenic controlsTectonics (subsidence, uplift), eustasy and climate. They are not independent: eustasy is itself driven by climate (ice volume) and tectonics (ocean-basin volume). Sediment supply is a product of tectonics and climate, not a fourth control of the same rank (Lecture 2, after Catuneanu, 2006).Act basin-wide, so their effects can be correlated. Autogenic processes (avulsion, lobe switching) act locally and do not.
2 Accommodation vs sedimentationRelative sea level sets the rate of creation or loss of accommodation; supply and energy flux set the rate of sedimentation. Supply is not sedimentation: energy flux decides how much of the supply stays.Decides the direction of the shoreline. A rising sea level does not guarantee a landward-moving shoreline.
3 Shoreline trajectoryLandward and up (transgression), seaward and up (normal regression), seaward and down (forced regression). Forced regression is driven by relative fall at the shoreline, whatever the supply.Sets the geometry of what is deposited next to the shoreline.
4 Stacking patternsRetrogradational (transgressive), progradational with aggradation (normal regressive, lowstand or highstand), progradational with downstepping (forced regressive). Where the shoreline is out of reach, upstream-controlled fluvial patterns (high vs low channel amalgamation).The basis for all units and surfaces: each type of stacking pattern defines a systems tract (Catuneanu, 2017).
5 SurfacesForm where and when the stacking pattern changes: onset of fall (BSFR), end of fall (CC), end of regression (MRS), end of transgression (MFS); plus erosion surfaces (SU, ravinement surfaces, RSME).Defined by a change in trend, not by a change in lithology. The same surface can be erosional in one place and conformable in another.
6 Systems tractsA linkage of contemporaneous depositional systems, forming the subdivision of a sequence (Brown & Fisher, 1977, in Catuneanu, 2019a): FSST, LST, TST, HST.Predicts where sands, muds, seals and coals sit relative to each other.
7 SequencesA succession of strata deposited during a full cycle of change in accommodation or sediment supply (Catuneanu et al., 2009), bounded by the same type of surface at base and top.The boundary surface is a choice; the rocks and the surfaces are not.
8 Wheeler diagramPlots each stratum where and when it was deposited (Wheeler, 1958).Makes gaps visible: hiatus, erosion, and the basinward passage of an unconformity into a conformable surface.

Two confusions this chain is meant to prevent

Maximum flooding surface vs sequence boundary. The MFS forms at the end of transgression, when the shoreline reaches its most landward position; it is the paleo-seafloor at that moment and separates a retrogradational package below from a highstand normal regressive package above (Catuneanu, 2017). The surfaces most often used as sequence boundaries (subaerial unconformity with the basal surface of forced regression or with the correlative conformity) form during and at the end of relative sea-level fall, the opposite end of the cycle. The MFS can be chosen as a sequence boundary (Galloway, 1989), but that is a convention; it never becomes a surface that formed during fall.

Normal vs forced regression. Both move the shoreline seaward. In normal regression the shoreline also moves up, because relative sea level is still rising and sedimentation outpaces it. In forced regression it moves down, because relative sea level falls at the shoreline. A seaward-moving shoreline alone does not tell you that sea level fell.

Part II: The Model-Independent Workflow

4. Observe first, label last

Several sequence models exist (depositional sequences II, III and IV, genetic stratigraphic sequence, transgressive–regressive sequence). They differ mainly in which surface they promote to "sequence boundary"; the stacking patterns and the surfaces are the same (Catuneanu, 2006; Catuneanu et al., 2011). Catuneanu et al. (2009, 2011) therefore defined the common ground of all approaches as a methodology that does not depend on the model, developed further in Catuneanu (2019a, 2022). This is the workflow used in the course:

  1. Observe the stacking patterns in your data: forced regressive, normal regressive (lowstand or highstand) and transgressive; upstream-controlled fluvial patterns where the shoreline is out of reach. Use local data from the basin under study.
  2. Map the surfaces that separate them: subaerial unconformity, basal surface of forced regression, correlative conformity, maximum regressive surface, maximum flooding surface, ravinement surfaces.
  3. Assemble systems tracts and sequences from the stacking patterns and their bounding surfaces, at the scale your data allow.
  4. Choose the boundary convention last. Which surface becomes the "sequence boundary" is a model-dependent choice, often guided by which surface is best mapped in the available data (Catuneanu, 2019a).

Why the controls are not part of the workflow

Catuneanu (2019a) states that the interpretation of the underlying controls may follow the construction of a sequence stratigraphic framework, but plays no role in the workflow itself; and that global standards, such as a global sea-level chart, should not be used to build a local framework, whereas global correlations can be tested once local frameworks exist. In practice: you never start from "sea level fell here at 15 Ma". You start from the geometry and facies in front of you. A sequence boundary is evidence of base-level fall, not of eustasy (Lecture 2).

5. Going deeper: the chain without a sea, and a surface that looks diagnostic but is not

Karoo Basin: sequences controlled by tectonics alone

The Balfour Formation (late Late Permian to earliest Triassic) is a fully fluvial succession that accumulated in the foredeep of the Karoo foreland basin, with no coeval marine environment within the preserved basin. Catuneanu & Elango (2001) recognised six fluvial depositional sequences separated by subaerial unconformities, with an average duration of 0.66 My (six cycles in 4 My) and no recorded climatic fluctuations. Sediment accumulated during flexural subsidence driven by orogenic loading; the bounding unconformities relate to stages of isostatic uplift during unloading. Within each sequence, fluvial style changes upward from higher- to lower-energy systems as the topographic slope decreases.

What it shows for the chain: link 1 can be tectonics alone; link 2 still works (accommodation created by subsidence, destroyed by uplift); links 3 to 5 are replaced by their upstream-controlled equivalents; links 6 to 8 apply unchanged. Tectonically driven accommodation is not an exotic special case.

Viking Formation: a sharp-based sandstone is not a systems tract

Sharp-based shoreface sandstones encased in shelf mudstones have been interpreted as late highstand, forced regressive, lowstand and transgressive deposits. MacEachern et al. (1999) argued, for the Viking Formation of the Joffre field (Alberta), that the sharp base alone is not diagnostic. Their criterion was positional: in weakly storm-influenced shorefaces, the regressive surface of erosion (forced regression) and the sequence boundary (lowstand) pass seaward into correlative conformities beyond fair-weather wave base. Where the basal discontinuity stays erosional even below facies deposited under fair-weather wave base, it was cut earlier, by ravinement during shoreface retreat, so the body is a transgressively incised shoreface.

What it shows for the chain: identifying a surface (link 5) requires knowing where it sits relative to the shoreline trajectory (link 3). The same lithological contact can belong to three different scenarios.

One sea level, two shoreline directions

Along the present Adriatic coast, the shoreline progrades south of the Po delta and retreats to the north under the same relative sea-level history. Sediment from the Po is carried south by longshore currents, so the sedimentation rate exceeds the rate of accommodation creation in the south but not in the north (Catuneanu, 2019a, Fig. 96). Highstand normal regression and transgression are coeval along one coastline: link 2 is evaluated locally, not basin-wide.

6. Worked example: walking a succession through the workflow

A shelf-margin section shows five units, from bottom to top. The shoreline trajectory of each has been reconstructed from clinoform geometry and facies:

  • Unit A: shoreline moves seaward and up; topset aggradation decreases upward (convex-up trajectory); gradationally based, coarsening-upward shorefaces.
  • Unit B: shoreline moves seaward and down; sharp-based, detached shoreface sands offlap basinward; no topsets preserved; the area landward is truncated by a fluvial erosion surface.
  • Unit C: shoreline moves seaward and up; topset aggradation increases upward (concave-up trajectory).
  • Unit D: shoreline moves landward and up; facies belts backstep; deepening-upward succession capped by a condensed, high-gamma mudstone.
  • Unit E: shoreline moves seaward and up again; convex-up trajectory.

Tasks. (a) Name the stacking pattern and systems tract of each unit. (b) Name the surface at each contact and the moment it records. (c) Where would the sequence boundary be placed in depositional sequence II, depositional sequence III/IV, the genetic stratigraphic sequence and the T–R sequence?

Show solution

(a) A: normal regression following a transgression, so highstand (HST). B: forced regression (FSST); offlap and downstepping require relative sea-level fall at the shoreline. C: normal regression following a forced regression, so lowstand (LST); the concave-up trajectory records accelerating rates of accommodation creation after the onset of rise (Catuneanu, 2017). D: transgression (TST). E: highstand normal regression (HST).

(b) A/B: basal surface of forced regression, the paleo-seafloor at the onset of forced regression; landward, the subaerial unconformity truncates it. B/C: correlative conformity, the paleo-seafloor at the end of forced regression, connecting landward with the downdip end of the subaerial unconformity. C/D: maximum regressive surface, end of regression, reworked in part by the transgressive surface of erosion where wave or tidal scour occurred. D/E: maximum flooding surface, end of transgression; the condensed high-gamma mudstone is where you look for it.

(c) Depositional sequence II: subaerial unconformity + basal surface of forced regression (A/B). Depositional sequences III and IV: subaerial unconformity + correlative conformity (B/C); they differ only in how they split the sequence into systems tracts. Genetic stratigraphic sequence: maximum flooding surface (D/E). T–R sequence: subaerial unconformity + marine part of the maximum regressive surface (C/D). Lecture 2, after Catuneanu (2006) and Catuneanu et al. (2011). Four conventions, one set of observations: if your answer to (a) and (b) changes with the model, step 1 of the workflow went wrong.

Interpretations must be justified from stacking patterns and shoreline trajectories, not from a model template (Lecture 2). In practice:

Weak justificationWhy it failsTrajectory-based justification
"Unit X is the HST because it lies above the MFS."Circular: the MFS is identified from the change in stacking pattern, so it cannot be the evidence for it."Unit X forestepps and upsteps after a backstepping unit, so it is a highstand normal regression; the contact between them is the MFS."
"Sea level fell, so there is a sequence boundary here."Starts from an assumed control instead of the record."Clinoforms downstep and offlap, and the topsets are truncated: the shoreline moved seaward and down, so relative sea level fell at the shoreline."
"This sandstone is sharp-based, so it is a forced regression."A sharp base occurs in several scenarios (MacEachern et al., 1999)."The shoreface sand rests on a basinward-younging erosion surface and the shoreline trajectory descends; the sharp base is a regressive surface of marine erosion."

Glossary of Core Terms

One-line genetic definitions: what the term records and when it forms. Abbreviations as used in the course and in the Wheeler tools. Definitions follow Catuneanu (2017, 2019a) unless stated.

TermGenetic definition
AccommodationSpace made available for sediment to fill, created or destroyed by relative sea-level change (eustasy plus subsidence or uplift); measured independently of sedimentation (Jervey, 1988).
Sedimentation vs supplySupply is the load delivered by transport agents; sedimentation is what accumulates at a place. Energy flux decides how much supply becomes sedimentation (Lecture 2, after Catuneanu, 2006).
Base levelSurface of equilibrium between sedimentation and erosion: below it there is space for sediment, above it the surface is eroded or bypassed.
Relative sea levelSea level measured from a datum attached to the substrate; changes with eustasy and with subsidence or uplift. Not the same as water depth.
Shoreline trajectoryThe path of the shoreline through time; the key element in defining stacking patterns in downstream-controlled settings (Catuneanu, 2017; trajectory analysis: Helland-Hansen & Hampson, 2009).
Transgression (T)Backstepping and upstepping of the shoreline; occurs when sediment supply cannot fill the accommodation created by relative sea-level rise at the shoreline.
Normal regression (NR)Forestepping and upstepping; occurs when sediment supply outpaces the accommodation created by relative sea-level rise at the shoreline. Lowstand if it follows a forced regression, highstand if it follows a transgression.
Forced regression (FR)Forestepping and downstepping; driven by relative sea-level fall at the shoreline, whatever the supply.
Stratal stacking patternThe architecture built by one type of shoreline trajectory (or, upstream, by channel amalgamation); each type defines a systems tract, and each change defines a surface.
Systems tractA linkage of contemporaneous depositional systems, forming the subdivision of a sequence; interpreted from stacking patterns, stratal relations and bounding surfaces; scale-independent.
FSSTFalling-stage systems tract: the forced regressive stacking pattern; built during relative sea-level fall at the shoreline.
LSTLowstand systems tract: normal regression after a forced regression, at the start of relative rise; typically a concave-up trajectory.
TSTTransgressive systems tract: the retrogradational stacking pattern; built while accommodation is created faster than sediment fills it at the shoreline.
HSTHighstand systems tract: normal regression after a transgression, late in the rise; typically a convex-up trajectory.
Subaerial unconformity (SU)Forms under subaerial conditions by fluvial erosion or bypass, pedogenesis, wind degradation or karstification; most commonly during negative accommodation, landward of the shoreline.
Basal surface of forced regression (BSFR)Paleo-seafloor at the onset of forced regression; separates normal regressive deposits below from forced regressive deposits above.
Correlative conformity (CC)Paleo-seafloor at the end of forced regression; separates forced regressive below from lowstand normal regressive above, and connects with the downdip end of the SU.
Maximum regressive surface (MRS)Paleo-seafloor at the end of regression, when the shoreline is at its most seaward position and turns landward; marks the change from regression to transgression.
Maximum flooding surface (MFS)Paleo-seafloor at the end of transgression, when the shoreline is at its most landward position; separates transgressive below from highstand normal regressive above. Often cryptic, inside a condensed section.
Transgressive surface of erosion (TSE, ravinement)Wave- or tidal-scour surface cut during transgression in coastal to shallow water; youngs landward and reworks part of the MRS.
Regressive surface of marine erosion (RSME)Wave-scour surface cut during forced regression as wave base is lowered by relative fall; youngs basinward, reworks part of the BSFR, overlain by sharp-based shorefaces.
Flooding surfaceA facies contact recording an abrupt increase in water depth; a lithological (allostratigraphic) surface that may or may not mark a change in stacking pattern. Not the same as an MFS.
SequenceA succession of strata deposited during a full cycle of change in accommodation or sediment supply (Catuneanu et al., 2009); bounded at base and top by the same type of sequence stratigraphic surface.
Wheeler diagramChronostratigraphic chart with time on the vertical axis and distance on the horizontal; shows deposition, hiatus and erosion where and when they occurred (Wheeler, 1958).

Key Points Summary

  • The course follows one chain: allogenic controls → accommodation vs sedimentation → shoreline trajectory → stacking patterns → surfaces → systems tracts → sequences → Wheeler diagram
  • The shoreline moves according to a balance of rates, not according to the sea-level curve alone
  • Surfaces are defined by changes in stacking pattern, that is by when and where they form, not by lithology
  • The workflow runs against the causal chain: observe stacking patterns, map surfaces, assemble tracts and sequences, choose the boundary convention last
  • Sequence models differ in packaging, not in the rocks; the MFS forms at the end of transgression, the SU/CC during and at the end of fall
  • Tectonics alone can drive full sequences (Karoo); a sharp base alone does not identify a systems tract (Viking)
  • Exam: Wheeler diagram plus three questions, justified from trajectories; assignments weekly, Sunday 12:00, +0.3 bonus at 80 %, only if you can explain them
